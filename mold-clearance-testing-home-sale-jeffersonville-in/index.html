Mold Clearance Testing Before a Home Sale in Jeffersonville, IN

By the Jeffersonville Water Damage Pros team

If a house had mold, buyers want proof that it was handled. Clearance testing is how that proof is built.

☎ Call (812) 642-7696

Few things slow a home sale like a mold surprise during inspection. A buyer who sees a stain or smells a musty basement may ask for a price cut, repairs or a walk-away. If the home had a past mold problem that was properly remediated, documentation can keep the sale on track.

Clearance testing is an independent check after remediation that the area is back to a normal condition. This guide explains the idea in plain language. It is general information, and your real estate agent or attorney should advise you on disclosure rules.

What clearance testing is

After a mold job, someone has to say it is done. If the contractor says so, there is a conflict of interest. Clearance testing uses a third party, often called an assessor or hygienist, who inspects the area and may take air or surface samples.

The assessor compares the results with outdoor air or with an unaffected area of the home and writes a report. The report states what was checked, how, and what was found. That document is what a buyer or lender can read.

A restoration company such as ours cleans and dries, and we can provide moisture readings and a written scope of work. We leave testing to an independent assessor, which is the point of having one. Our mold remediation page describes the cleanup side.

Documents worth keeping

  • The original scope of work and photos before and after.
  • Dated moisture readings showing the area dried.
  • The assessor's clearance report, if one was done.
  • Invoices for repairs and for the plumbing or roof fix that stopped the source.
  • Any paperwork the repair company gives you.

Buyers and their inspectors want to see that the source of the moisture was fixed and not just the surface. A pile of paperwork that shows the leak repair, the drying and the cleanup answers most of the questions before they are asked.

Timing and expectations

Testing is usually done after cleaning and drying are complete and before new drywall or paint closes things up, or just after. Ask your assessor when they prefer to visit. Results can take a few days, so schedule early if you have a closing date.

Testing does not mean mold will never return. If moisture returns, growth can too. What it shows is the condition at the time of the test. Keep humidity in check after the sale as well, and give buyers the same advice.

If you are the buyer

  1. Ask the seller whether there was ever water damage or mold.
  2. Request repair records and any clearance report.
  3. Have your inspector check the basement, attic and crawl space for moisture.
  4. Ask for moisture readings on any area that looks stained.
  5. Talk to your agent about contract terms if problems are found.

A past mold problem that was properly handled is not necessarily a reason to walk away. An undocumented one deserves more questions.

Questions sellers often ask

Sellers worry that bringing up past mold will scare buyers. In practice, an honest record of a fixed problem usually helps more than silence, because inspectors tend to find stains and smells anyway.

  • Disclose according to the rules your agent explains to you.
  • Present the repair paperwork up front, not after the inspection.
  • Explain what caused the moisture and how it was fixed.
  • Offer access to the basement, crawl space and attic for inspection.

If you have not had the problem addressed yet, address it before listing. A clean, dry space photographs and inspects better than one with a fresh stain. It is also easier to answer a buyer's questions when the work is already done and the paperwork is in a folder.

If you are unsure whether a smell or stain is a real problem, a phone call to describe it can help you decide who to bring in first.

A timeline to plan around

  1. Fix the moisture source and document the repair.
  2. Dry the area and record moisture readings.
  3. Complete remediation and cleaning.
  4. Arrange the independent inspection and any sampling.
  5. Rebuild, paint and finish.
  6. Collect the paperwork into one folder for the listing.

Starting at the top of this list as soon as you notice a problem keeps the sale schedule realistic.

Selling in Jeffersonville and nearby

Jeffersonville's housing runs from older homes in the historic district and near downtown to newer subdivisions farther out. Basements and crawl spaces in older homes are the places buyers most often ask about.

If your home is near the Ohio River or in a Special Flood Hazard Area, the city notes that flood insurance may be required with a federally backed mortgage. Expect questions about past flooding and have your records ready.

Frequently asked questions

Is clearance testing required to sell a house?

Not as a general rule that we know of. It is a way to document the condition. Ask your agent or attorney about disclosure rules.

Who pays for clearance testing?

Usually the property owner who is selling or the one who hired the remediation. This is a matter for you and your contract.

Can the remediation company do the testing?

It is better practice for an independent party to test, so the result is not marked by the company that did the work.

Does a clean report mean mold cannot come back?

No. It shows the condition when tested. Moisture control is what keeps it away.

What if a buyer finds mold during inspection?

Negotiations vary. Get the source fixed, document the cleanup and talk to your agent about options.
